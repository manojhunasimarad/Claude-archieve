How to read a paper, and what to mark while you do it

You are not reading for comprehension. You are reading to fill twelve fields in TEMPLATE.md — and to notice, across sixty papers, the thing the field keeps failing to measure. Every mark you make has a destination.

01

Never read front to back

A paper is not a story and reading it linearly is the slowest possible way to extract it. Three passes, each with a decision at the end. Most papers stop after the second — deep-reading everything is a way of feeling productive while covering a quarter of the ground.

1 Triage 5 min

Title, abstract, conclusion, figures, section headings. Nothing else. Decision: read · queue · exclude. An exclusion needs a written reason.

2 Structural 20 min

Problem, method, experimental setup, headline results, stated limitations. Enough to write §1–§3, §5 and §7 and assign roles. This is where most papers end.

3 Deep 1–3 hrs · selective

Only for foundational, directly competitive, or reproduction targets. Derivations, assumptions, experimental validity — and what the evaluation fails to measure.

The arithmetic

60 summaries · 7 weeks · roughly 9 per week. At 25 minutes each that is under four hours a week. It only works if you stop at Pass 2 by default and spend Pass 3 deliberately, on maybe six papers total.

02

Four markers, four destinations

The rule that makes highlighting worth doing: a mark you cannot file is a mark you should not have made. Each colour belongs to one section of the template. When you sit down to write, you are transcribing, not re-reading.

Two to four per paper. Transcribe verbatim with the page number the moment you mark it. A quotation reconstructed later from memory is a paraphrase wearing quotation marks.

The mistake to avoid

Pasting the abstract into §1. Verbatim text goes to §10 with a location, always. §1 is your paragraph, in their framing. Text that starts as an unmarked copy-paste is how accidental plagiarism appears six weeks later, when you no longer remember which words were yours.

03

The audit that is actually yours

Seven axes, rated on every paper: measured · modelled · assumed away · ignored. Most ML papers never apply this lens, which is precisely why it is worth applying. Four years of making software run on constrained automotive silicon is not a footnote here — it is the reason you can see what a machine-learning reader cannot.

AxisWhat counts as measuredUsual reality
LatencyWall-clock, reported with varianceRound count only
Energy / powerJoules or watts on real hardwareIgnored
BandwidthBytes on the wire per roundModelled
MemoryPeak resident on the client deviceIgnored
Determinism / real-timeDeadlines stated and metAlmost always absent
ScaleClient count varied, not fixed at 10Simulated
Fault toleranceDropout / failure injectedAssumed away
Discipline

One paper omitting determinism is an observation. Five independent papers omitting it is evidence, and only then may a gap be proposed. Record the rating, resist the conclusion.

1

Read the title — every word is load-bearing

A Survey of Federated Learning for Connected and Automated Vehicles

A Survey of
The paper type. No new method, no experiments of its own. Expect a taxonomy table and a challenges section — and read those two first.
Federated Learning
The method family being surveyed.
for
Direction of application. FL is being applied to vehicles — vehicles are the domain, not the contribution.
Connected and Automated
Not "autonomous". Connected is doing work: without connectivity there is no federation. The word tells you connectivity is assumed throughout.

Set Roles: survey in your header block. That single decision changes how you read the rest — §5 Evaluation will be N/A, and §3 will be a taxonomy rather than a method.

2

Read the author block — this one matters more than usual

Four authors, all at College of Engineering, Purdue University. One group, one laboratory, one point of view.

Catch this now

Your index already holds two Chellapandi surveys — chellapandi2023survey and chellapandi2023federated — with overlapping authors from the same lab. G1 wants ≥2 surveys and they both count. Stage 2 of the gap protocol wants five independent papers, and these are one opinion expressed twice.

Note it in §11 now. Finding this in week two costs a sentence; finding it during a gap proposal costs a fortnight.

3

Read the abstract — one sentence at a time, each one has a job

An abstract is six sentences doing six different jobs. Learn to recognise them and you can triage any paper in ninety seconds.

"Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) are one of the emerging technologies… leading to a safe, efficient, and sustainable transportation system."

Job: motivation. Boilerplate. Every CAV paper opens this way. Read fast, mark nothing.

"…machine learning models in CAVs are solely trained using the local vehicle data, and the performance is not certain when exposed to new environments or unseen conditions."

Job: the problem. This is your §1. Local-only training generalises badly. Green — this is quotable.

"Federated learning (FL) is an effective solution… enables a collaborative model development with multiple vehicles in a distributed learning framework."

Job: the proposed answer. Note "effective" is asserted, not shown — a survey inherits its field's optimism.

"FL enables CAVs to learn from a wide range of driving environments and improve their overall performance while ensuring the privacy and security of local vehicle data."

Job: the two claimed benefits — generalisation and privacy. Every paper in this cluster will claim one or both. Worth remembering as the field's standard pitch.

"In this paper, we review the progress accomplished by researchers in applying FL to CAVs."

Job: scope. Confirms survey.

"A broader view of the various data modalities and algorithms… Specific applications of FL are reviewed in detail, and an analysis of the challenges and future scope…"

Job: the map. This is the trick — the last abstract sentence names the sections. Modalities and algorithms → §II. Applications → §III. Challenges → §IV. You now know where everything is without reading the contents page.

4

Jump to Table II, page 5 — the taxonomy, and your §3

Skip §I and §II for now. Table II is the densest thing in the paper: thirteen studies from 2020–2023 across six columns.

Read the FL Algorithm column twice. Almost everything is FedAvg or a light variant. That single observation tells you the field's methodological centre of gravity — and any paper you eventually write will be compared against FedAvg.

5

Go to §IV, page 6 — the challenges, and your §7

One page. Eight bolded lead sentences, each a named challenge. Highlight every bolded lead — four minutes, and most of §7 is done.

  • B · Imperfect methodology — privacy and security · no robust vehicle selection or resource allocation · catastrophic forgetting · no fairness or incentive mechanism
  • C · Inadequate evaluation — new vehicles cannot judge FL suitability · no high-capability diagnostics for faulty sensors

Reuse these codes on the next paper wherever they fit. A new code should mean a genuinely new theme, not a rephrasing — inflating the vocabulary keeps the G2 curve artificially high and hides the moment the field stops surprising you.

6

Now the blue pass over §IV — and what only you will see

Run the systems marker through that same page. It names solve time, memory utilization, computational power, communication costs.

It never once names determinism, real-time deadlines, or energy — in a survey about vehicles, where compute is a certified, mixed-criticality, hard-real-time platform, and where a training job competing with a perception job has a safety consequence.

Rate Determinism / real-time as ignored in §6 and write the evidence: "§IV enumerates resource limits but names no timing or energy constraint." Then move to the next paper and see whether it happens again.

7

Backfill §1 and §2 from the front matter — five minutes

Only now go back to page 1. You know what the paper does, so §I reads in a fraction of the time. Two facts there are worth keeping: vehicles generate 1–2 TB per vehicle per day, and GDPR is why centralised collection is not an option. Together they are the entire justification for federation, in two numbers.

Glance at Table I (p. 2) — it compares on-vehicle, centralised and federated learning on eight features. That is your one-paragraph answer to "why federated rather than centralised", and it is worth a green mark.

Fig. 1 (p. 2) is the system picture: each vehicle senses, trains locally, sends a model to the server; the server aggregates and sends a general model back. If you can redraw that from memory, you understand FL.

Every term in this paper, decoded

Nothing here should be a word you skim past. Most of it recurs in every paper in the cluster, so this is a one-time cost.

Notation — how a citation points at a place in a paper

Every quotation you record needs a location, so that you or an examiner can go back and check it. That location is written in a compact shorthand. Take one apart:

Chellapandi et al. 2023 · §IV-A, p. 6

Chellapandi
Surname of the first author only.
et al.
Latin et alii, "and others". Used when there are three or more authors, so you name the first and abbreviate the rest.
2023
Year of publication.
§
The section sign. Read it aloud as "section". §IV means "Section 4".
IV
Roman numeral 4. Papers in this venue number their sections I, II, III, IV, V rather than 1, 2, 3. I=1 · II=2 · III=3 · IV=4 · V=5 · VI=6 · VII=7 · VIII=8 · IX=9 · X=10
-A
Subsection A within that section. So §IV-A is "Section 4, subsection A" — in this paper, Resource Limitations.
p. 6
Page 6. pp. 6–8 would mean pages 6 to 8. The full stop is part of the abbreviation.

So §IV-A, p. 6 means: Section 4, subsection A, printed page 6. Write it that way and anyone can find the sentence in under ten seconds.

[12]
A numbered reference — look up entry 12 in the reference list at the end.
Fig. · Tab.
Figure and Table. Fig. 1 is the first figure.
arXiv:2303.
10677v1
An arXiv identifier. 2303 = March 2023, then the sequence number, then v1 = version 1. Later versions exist as v2, v3 — always record which one you read.
DOI
Digital Object Identifier — a permanent link to a published work. Prefer it over a URL, which rots.

Federated learning — the core machinery

FL
Federated Learning. Train a shared model across many devices without moving their data. Coined by Google, first shipped in Gboard keyboard prediction.
CL
Centralised Learning. The alternative: ship all data to one server. Blocked here by volume and privacy law.
Client
One participating device. Here, one vehicle.
Local model
The copy each client trains on its own data.
Global model
The single model the server maintains by combining client updates.
Aggregation
The server's combining step — usually a weighted average of client weights.
Comm. round
One full cycle: server sends model → clients train → clients send updates → server aggregates. Progress is counted in rounds, not epochs.
FedAvg
The default algorithm. Clients run several local SGD steps, then the server averages their weights, weighted by dataset size. Fewer rounds than sending every gradient.
FedSGD
Stricter variant — one gradient step per round. More rounds, more communication.
FedProx
FedAvg plus a penalty that stops a client drifting too far from the global model. Helps when clients' data differ.
Async FL
Asynchronous — the server does not wait for all clients. Directly relevant to slow or intermittently connected vehicles.
Heterogeneous
Clients differ — in data, sensors, or compute. The central difficulty of the whole field.
non-IID
Not independent and identically distributed: each vehicle sees its own biased slice of the world. Averaging models trained on different distributions degrades the result.
Personalisation
Adapting the global model back to one client's conditions.

Models and training

SGD
Stochastic Gradient Descent. Step the weights downhill on a small batch at a time.
Epoch
One full pass over a client's local data.
Learning rate η
How big each downhill step is.
Loss function
The number being minimised — how wrong the model currently is.
Convergence
The point where more rounds stop improving the model.
CNN
Convolutional Neural Network — the standard architecture for images.
RNN / LSTM
Recurrent nets for sequences; LSTM handles long-range dependencies. Used for trajectory and vehicle-state time series.
RL / Deep RL
Reinforcement Learning — learn by trial and error against an environment. Used here for collision avoidance.
YOLO, ResNet,
U-Net, LeNet-5
Named CNN architectures appearing in Table II. Treat as labels; you do not need their internals to summarise this paper.

The vehicle

CAV
Connected and Automated Vehicle.
ECU
Electronic Control Unit — an in-vehicle computer. You have shipped software on these.
V2X
Vehicle-to-Everything communication.
GNSS
Satellite positioning (GPS is one GNSS).
LiDAR
Laser ranging; outputs a 3D point cloud. Accurate in bad weather, but large and expensive to transmit.
RADAR
Radio ranging. Robust, coarse.
IMU
Inertial Measurement Unit — accelerometers and gyroscopes.
Modality
A kind of sensor data: image, LiDAR point cloud, vehicle status time series. "Multi-modal" means several at once.
Perception
Turning sensor data into an understanding of the scene.
Semantic
segmentation
Labelling every pixel by what it belongs to — road, car, pedestrian.

Privacy and security

GDPR
EU data protection regulation. The legal reason centralised collection fails.
HE
Homomorphic Encryption — compute on encrypted data without decrypting it.
SMPC
Secure Multi-Party Computation — several parties jointly compute without revealing their inputs.
DP
Differential Privacy — add calibrated noise so no individual record can be recovered. Costs accuracy.
Secure
aggregation
Server learns the sum of client updates but not any single one.
Blockchain
Decentralised ledger, proposed here to remove the trusted central server.

# chellapandi2023survey

**Cluster:** sdv-automotive · **Roles:** survey · **Tier:** P · **Relevance:** 4/5

## 1 · Problem
ML in CAVs is trained only on one vehicle's own data, so behaviour in
unseen conditions is unpredictable. Pooling the data centrally is not
available: vehicles produce 1-2 TB/day each, and GDPR-class regulation
forbids moving it. FL is offered as the way to train across a fleet
without the data leaving the vehicle.

## 2 · Core idea
A survey, not a method. Organises FL-for-CAV along four axes - data
modality, security technique, FL algorithm, application - and closes
with a three-part challenge taxonomy. Its value here is Table II and
Section IV, not its argument.

## 3 · Method
Table II, p.5 - 13 studies, 2020-2023, six columns:
  Literature | Year | Data Modality | Application | Base Model |
  FL Algorithm | Dataset
Dominant values: RGB image; steering/detection/driver-monitoring;
CNN variants + LSTM; FedAvg in nearly every row.
Security techniques (II-B): HE, SMPC, DP, blockchain.

## 4 · Assumptions
- Data distribution: heterogeneity acknowledged; non-IID never
  formally treated
- Device capability: vehicles can train locally - no compute,
  memory or thermal budget is ever stated
- Network: connectivity continuously available
- Availability: vehicles reachable each round
- Trust: server honest-but-curious; attacks noted in II-B
- Scale: "massively parallel" asserted, never quantified
- Other (inferred, unstated): training runs on the vehicle without
  contending with safety-critical workloads for CPU or memory

## 5 · Evaluation
N/A - survey. Field-standard datasets recorded in Table II; accuracy
is the near-universal metric. No paper in the table reports latency
or energy.

## 6 · Systems-constraints audit
| Axis        | Rating       | Evidence                              |
| Latency     | modelled     | "solve time" IV-A, never measured     |
| Energy      | ignored      | absent from the paper entirely        |
| Bandwidth   | modelled     | comm. cost discussed II-A, IV-A       |
| Memory      | modelled     | "memory utilization" IV-A             |
| Determinism  | ignored      | IV lists resource limits, no timing
| Scale       | modelled     | "massively parallel", unquantified    |
| Fault tol.  | modelled     | diagnostics named as a need, IV-C     |

## 7 · Limitations
Stated (IV): resource cost at scale · no real-world datasets or
simulator · privacy/security · no robust client selection ·
catastrophic forgetting · no incentive mechanism · cold-start
vehicles · no fault diagnostics.
Exhibited: treats the vehicle as a generic edge device. No
engagement with automotive compute as a certified, mixed-criticality,
real-time platform.

**Limitation codes:** sim-only | no-real-world-data | comm-cost |
no-client-selection | catastrophic-forgetting | no-incentive-mechanism |

## 9 · Relevance here
Rules IN the systems angle: this field measures accuracy and
communication, not time or energy, and does not model the platform the
training actually runs on. Rules OUT "apply FL to vehicles" as novelty

Your protocol was not invented from nothing — it is an unusually strict assembly of four established methods. Reading the originals is worth two hours, because each one explains why a gate exists, and each is citable in the methodology chapter you will eventually have to write. All four below are free.

Keshav — How to Read a Paper ACM SIGCOMM CCR 37(3), 2007 · free PDF

Four pages. The origin of the three-pass method in §01, from fifteen years of doing it at Waterloo. Its second half — using the same method to conduct a literature survey — is the part most people never reach, and it is directly about what you are doing now.

Why to skip the gap-finding advice specifically

General advice defines a literature gap as "an area that has not been studied, or lacks substantial inquiry". That is written for someone with no system at all, and it is close to what your own gap-validation-protocol.md lists as its first anti-pattern: "No one has applied X to Y — novelty of combination is not contribution."

Your bar is five independent papers, a documented counter-evidence search, an adversarial self-review and pre-committed kill criteria. Generic advice would let you declare a gap in an afternoon. You already have the stricter method; do not trade down to the popular one. That is not a criticism of the channel — it is aimed at a much broader audience than a supervised M.Tech with a script-checked protocol.

The one hard rule about AI extraction tools

Use them to find papers. Never to fill a summary.

Your citation-integrity.md requires that quotations be transcribed from the source and that metadata never be filled from memory — and a model's memory is exactly what a generated summary is. A plausible sentence with a page number that nobody checked is the single most expensive kind of error in a thesis, because it survives every review until an examiner opens the paper. §4, §6, §7 and §10 are written by you, with the PDF open. That is the whole method.

08

The loop, per paper

  1. Triage, 5 min. Abstract, conclusion, figures, headings. Decide.
  2. Copy the template to summaries/<key>.md, fill the header block.
  3. Structural pass, 20 min, four markers live. Mark as you go, never after.
  4. Transcribe — each colour into its section. §4, §6, §7, §10.
  5. Assign limitation codes, reusing existing ones wherever they fit.
  6. Set status to summarized in index.csv, add the summary path.
  7. Run python -m tools.lab lit check, then lit gates.

Watch lit gates move. It is the only honest signal that the review is progressing, and far more motivating than a page count — a cluster crossing G1 is a real event.
